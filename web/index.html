How it works

Three steps from orbit to a rescue list.

  1. The radar looks

    NISAR L-band radar images the city every 12 days, day or night, through clouds.

  2. We measure the change

    We compare image pairs from before and across the quake. A strong loss of radar coherence over buildings is an indicator of possible damage.

  3. We rank the districts

    Share of buildings with a changed signal × residents = people potentially affected. Areas are ranked for inspection.

Research

Both cities have been here before.

26 April 1966

The Tashkent earthquake

  • A magnitude 5.1–5.3 shock at 05:23, only 3–8 km under the city centre.2
  • About 100,000 people lost their homes. Other estimates say 200,000 to 300,000.1,2
  • 28,000 buildings destroyed, including 200 hospitals and clinics and 180 schools.1
  • Reported deaths range from 10 to 200. By 1970 the city had 100,000 new homes.1,2

This is history, not satellite data. NISAR launched in 2025.

3.16M

Seismic risk in Uzbekistan

  • That many people live in Tashkent's districts. The whole city is in the 8–9 intensity zone (MSK-64).8
  • More than half of the country's people and economy are in areas of high seismic risk.3
  • In an average year earthquakes affect about 1 million people in Uzbekistan.4
  • A World Bank study puts Tashkent first for seismic hazard among the nine largest cities of Central Asia and the Caucasus.5

The city was also damaged by earthquakes in 1866 and 1886.2

M 7.4

Pereira, Colombia: our test case

  • The 10 August 2026 earthquake is the strongest recorded in Colombia this century, and Pereira was one of the hardest-hit cities.11,12
  • In 1999 an Mw 6.2 earthquake hit Armenia and Pereira: 1,171 deaths, about 5,000 seriously injured and up to 250,000 people left homeless.9
  • In 1995 an Mw 6.4 earthquake badly affected Pereira, and many buildings there were rebuilt or retrofitted afterwards.9,10

We chose Pereira because it has both NISAR passes after the quake and an official Copernicus damage map to check against.

About every 3 days

How NISAR works

  • NISAR is a NASA and ISRO radar satellite launched on 30 July 2025, the first with both L-band and S-band radar.6,7
  • It flies 747 km up and works at night and through clouds. Its orbit repeats every 12 days, but because it images on both ascending and descending passes, it samples a place about every 6 days on average.6,13
  • NISAR is not the only radar satellite in orbit. Adding ESA's Sentinel-1 satellites (every 6–12 days) means a fresh radar image of a city can arrive about every 3 days (our estimate).14
  • Radar sends its own signal and records the echo. Where nothing changed, two passes match (high coherence). Collapse and debris break the match, but so can construction or vegetation, so a coherence drop is an indicator of possible damage, not proof.
